Lesson 01Why you're here

Everyone is offering AI services now.

It is getting noisy. Hard to tell who to follow, and harder to stand out when you are competing with people running the same demo you are.

Where the edge is

An accountant who has audited real books knows what a good budget looks like, and knows the places people get it wrong. The model does not have that.

Which is why tenure helps here. You already know how to decide, and deciding is the expensive part.

Lesson 01Why you're here

I stopped writing proposals. I build the thing, show it, and ask if they want to pay for it.

It is a lot easier than explaining what I would have built.

Lesson 02Tools

Tools don't matter.

I am saying that as someone addicted to Claude Code.

What carried over from every system I have ever wired together was never the platform. It was learning what an API call is, and how to read an error instead of panicking at it.

That is why I know what to watch out for now.

Lesson 02Tools

When Claude Code goes down I switch to Codex.

Same context, same files, I keep working. I never re-brief anybody.

Everything the AI needs to know about me and my business is written down in one place, in plain files I own.

Why it matters

You stop waiting for the perfect tool, and you stop panicking when your favorite one gets replaced.

Lesson 03Being AI native

Being AI native is what you reach for first.

Something lands on your desk. Your first move is either to try AI on it, or to do it the way you have always done it. That is the whole difference.

Lesson 03Being AI native

The question changed.

We used to ask

Can AI do this?

A yes or no gate, usually answered from memory, based on something you tried a year ago.

Now I ask

To what extent can AI do this?

If it handles 75%, that is a win. If it handles 9%, that is still a win, because you are ahead of the person doing all of it by hand.

These tools are the worst they will ever be. Whatever it could not do last quarter, try it again this month.

Lesson 04AI isn't the valuable part

We are all using the same model.

Everyone assumes the prize goes to whoever has the best prompt, the newest model, the fanciest skills. Any of us can open Opus 5 this afternoon.

So why don't we all get the same results?

Everyone brings a different brain to it.

Lesson 04AI isn't the valuable part

Six months of teaching it my mistakes.

Every day it audits what I did. The next day it levels up from what it found.

The more I got wrong, the more there was to teach it.

What my machine knows now

The ways a website build goes wrong. What breaks in a back-office system. Which guardrails to put up, and what I always forget to prepare on security and privacy.

Lesson 04AI isn't the valuable part

Most of what I write down is what not to do.

My skills, my systems and my instructions are full of don'ts. That list is my failures, written down where the machine can read them.

A beginner has no way to fake it.

Anthropic writes it this way too

“Don't add features beyond what was asked for.”

“Don't add error handling or validation for scenarios that can't happen.”

This has a name. Context engineering.

Lesson 04AI isn't the valuable part

My definition

Context engineering is everything you put in around the model.

Your knowledge, your prompts, your instructions, your skills, the systems you build around it. It is how you apply your own brain onto a powerful AI model.

Lesson 05Managing

Stop talking to it. Start managing it.

Most people type a request, get an output, feel let down, and decide the model is not smart enough.

I hand it a problem instead. “I want to increase revenue for my business.” Then I make it tell me how it would go about that, and make it ask me questions until I am sure it understood what I meant.

Before it builds anything

If it has not asked you anything, it does not understand the problem yet.

Lesson 05Managing

It is trained to please you.

Ask what it thinks of your plan and it will tell you the plan is great. Every plan has blind spots, and that one will agree with all of yours.

The pre-mortem

Tell it the plan already failed. Give it nothing else. Make it work out every reason why, then make it tell you how each one could have been avoided.

The red team

Run the same plan past a skeptical customer, a competitor and an engineer. The customer is looking for the fatal flaw, the competitor for the upside you missed, the engineer for who maintains it in a year.

Lesson 05Managing

Give it a finish line.

A goal, plus the condition that tells it to stop.

“Build a production-ready onboarding flow. Done means design and engineering could pick it up tomorrow and build it.”

Then it keeps going until that is true.

Then you delegate

One session for ideas, one for planning, one for writing, one for building, one for verifying. Each with its own finish line.

It becomes an employee that can hire its own staff, and it will only be as good as you are at managing it.

Lesson 06Verify everything

You ask for done. You get seventy percent.

So you give feedback and it fixes one thing. You give more feedback and it fixes another. You claw your way up to ninety that way, one round at a time.

The question

What if it checked its own work, and kept checking until the thing was actually finished and it could show you why it was confident?

For anyone building agents, this is the one that matters most.

Lesson 06Verify everything

The setup is simpler than you think.

Ask yourself what you would do to check the work. Would you look at it? Would you test it? Would you run the signup yourself and see what happens?

Whatever that is, it can do it.

What it can already do

Drive a browser. Run tests. Read its own output back. Come at the same thing from a few different angles and tell you where they disagree.

Lesson 06Verify everything

Every site I build goes through a screenshot loop.

It takes the screenshots and checks the balance itself. Checks it on mobile. Then it clicks every button, submits every form, and confirms the formatting held.

All the things I used to squint at myself.

Start making your AI verify the work you do. Make it prove to you that the work is complete.

Lesson 07Grant access carefully

If it can reach something, assume it will eventually use it.

Handing an agent a tool and telling it not to touch that tool is handing someone a credit card and asking them not to spend. One day they spend.

Lesson 07Grant access carefully

You can tell it to only write drafts.

It will write drafts. Then one day it sends one, because the send tool was sitting there the whole time.

The prompt layer

Where you write the rules. The model reads them and mostly follows them.

The tool layer

Where you decide what it can physically do. Take the send tool away and it cannot send, whatever the prompt says.

A rule that lives in the prompt is a suggestion. A rule that lives in the tool is a restriction.

Lesson 07Grant access carefully

Run the same prompt a hundred times, get a hundred results.

Swap the model and it comes at the problem differently again. This is why a written rule holds only most of the time.

Keys work the same way

Hand over your Google Workspace and it has your calendar. If it can create events, assume it will create events.

Go through every tool, every database, every file, every key. Then answer one question about each: can this thing send, or can it only draft?

Lesson 08One success is not proof

Your agent worked once.

You have proven it worked one time, on one output. The model is non-deterministic, so your real success rate is a range, and you have not measured it yet.

The fix

Evals. Run it many times against cases you already know the answer to, and score what comes back.

Part 4 · Setting it up

The one rule I will not bend

Paste your writing. Do not type it.

When it asks for two things you have written, open a real email and paste it raw. Typos included.

Anything you compose inside the chat is already shaped by the chat. Type it fresh and you teach it a voice that is not yours.
