POST205 · CRASH COURSE

The best thing you bring to AI is the part you already know.

Six lessons. Fifteen minutes. Everything I have figured out so far, including the parts I got wrong.

Who this is for
People who already know their work
What you need
A laptop. Curiosity. No coding.
The promise
AI makes you better at what you are
After this
You build your own system

Where I'm coming from

I read the thinking.

When I run a command, I don't skip to the answer. I read what the model was reasoning through on the way there. Line by line. Like a weirdo.

That habit taught me more than any course did.

The actual prerequisite

Curiosity. Not talent, not a technical background. If you want to know how the thing works and you are willing to poke at it, you are already qualified. If you only want the output, you will stay at the shallow end forever.

Lesson 01 · Why you're here

There are two doors in.

Door one

Start your own thing

Build with AI, find clients, sell the result. This is the door I went through.

Door two

Become the AI person where you already are

Same skills, aimed inward. The person in the company who actually knows how to make this work.

Both are good doors. I came in through systems and automation, and then AI started automating faster than the systems I was building by hand.

Lesson 01 · Why you're here

The room is getting loud.

Everyone is offering AI services now. Hard to know who to follow, harder to stand out, because you are competing with people doing the exact same demo.

What cuts through is knowing a business well enough to name its real problems.

Where the edge actually is

An accountant who has audited real books will build a better budgeting tool than someone who has never opened a spreadsheet. They know what good looks like. They know where people mess it up.

Deep domain knowledge is the rare part. The AI is not.

Which is why this is a good moment for people with tenure. You already know how to decide. That is the expensive skill.

Lesson 01 · Why you're here

Nobody buys the process. They buy the thing that is already working in front of them.

I stopped writing proposals. I build the thing, show it, and ask if they want to pay for it. It closes faster and it is a more honest conversation.

Lesson 02 · Tools

Tools don't matter. I say that as an addict.

I use Claude Code every day. I would still tell you the tool is the least valuable thing in the room.

What carried over from every system I have ever wired together was never the platform. It was knowing what an API call is, where things break, and how to read an error instead of panicking at it.

That knowledge is why I now know what to watch out for. No tool gave me that. Breaking things did.

Lesson 02 · Tools

Context is the part you keep.

Everything your AI needs to know about you and your business, written down in one place, in plain files you own.

When Claude Code goes down, I switch to Codex and keep working. Same context, same files. I never re-brief anyone.

Why this matters

You stop waiting for the perfect tool, and you stop panicking when your favorite one gets replaced. Both will happen. Neither should cost you a week.

The skill underneath all of it: communicate clearly, break the problem down, and find another way in when the first attempt flops.

Lesson 03 · Being AI native

Being AI native isn't how much you know. It's what you reach for first.

Something lands on your desk. Your hand moves before you think. Does it move toward the old way, or does it move toward "I wonder if AI can do this"? That reflex is the whole thing.

Lesson 03 · Being AI native

Ask a better question.

The old question

Can AI do this?

A yes-or-no gate. Most of the time you answer it wrong, from memory, based on something you tried a year ago.

The question now

To what extent can AI do this?

If it handles 75%, that is a clear win. If it handles 9%, that is still a win, because you are ahead of the person doing all of it by hand.

And these tools are the worst they will ever be. Whatever it could not do last quarter, try it again this month.

Lesson 04 · AI isn't the valuable part

We are all using the same model.

Everyone thinks the prize goes to whoever has the best prompt, the newest model, the fanciest skills.

Every one of us can open the same model today. So why doesn't everyone get the same results?

Because everyone brings a different brain to it.

Lesson 04 · AI isn't the valuable part

Six months of teaching it my mistakes.

Every day it audits what I did, and the next day it levels up from what it found.

More mistakes made means more learnings captured. The errors are the raw material.

What my machine knows now

The ways a website build goes wrong. What breaks in a back-office system. Which guardrails to put up, what to check on security and privacy, and what I always forget to prepare.

None of that came from the model. All of it came from me getting it wrong first.

Lesson 04 · AI isn't the valuable part

Most of what I write down is what not to do.

My skills, my systems, my instructions are full of don'ts. That list is my failures, written down where the machine can read them.

A beginner cannot fake that list. It only comes from having already broken the thing.

Even Anthropic writes it this way

“Don't add features beyond what was asked for.”

“Don't add error handling or validation for scenarios that can't happen.”

All of this has a name. Context engineering.

Lesson 04 · AI isn't the valuable part

My definition

Everyone runs the same model inside the same app. Context engineering is everything you put into it.

Your knowledge, your prompts, your instructions, your skills, the system you build around it. It is how you press your own brain onto a very powerful, very generic machine.

Lesson 05 · Managing

Stop talking to it. Start managing it.

Most people type a request, get an output, feel let down, and decide the model isn't smart enough.

I hand it a problem instead. “I want to increase revenue for my business.” Then I make it tell me how it would approach that, and make it ask me questions until I am sure it understands what I actually meant.

Before it builds anything

The questions it asks you are worth more than the first answer it gives you. If it hasn't asked you anything, it hasn't understood the problem. It has just guessed politely.

Lesson 05 · Managing

It is trained to please you.

Ask what it thinks of your plan and it will tell you the plan is great. Every plan has blind spots, and that one will agree with all of yours.

The pre-mortem

Tell it the plan already failed. Give it nothing else. Make it work out every reason why, then make it tell you how each one could have been avoided.

The red team

Run the same plan past a skeptical customer, a competitor, and an engineer. The customer finds the fatal flaw, the competitor finds the upside you missed, the engineer asks who maintains it in a year.

Lesson 05 · Managing

Give it a finish line, then let it run.

Not a prompt. A goal, plus the condition that tells it to stop.

“Build a production-ready onboarding flow. Done means design and engineering could pick it up tomorrow and build it.”

Then it keeps going until that is true.

Then you delegate

One session for ideas. One for planning. One for writing, one for building, one for verifying. Each with its own finish line.

It becomes an employee who can hire its own staff. It will only ever be as good as you are at managing them.

Part 2 · What we're building

The one test that matters

While you are away from your desk, it notices one real event and produces something faster and more accurate than what you would have made yourself.

Everything we build today points at that sentence. If a step does not move you toward it, we skip the step.

Part 2 · What we're building

How you'll know it's working

One

Someone asks you a question, and you ask your AIOS too. Because it answers better, with the actual source.

Two

You stop opening tabs. Something lands and your first move is to ask, instead of hunting across six apps.

Three

You stop trying to remember. You trust the retrieval. It holds the facts, you hold the questions.

None of these are metrics. You feel all three, and you feel them in the same month.

Part 3 · The Shift

Question · Cut · Build

  1. 1

    Question

    To what extent can AI do this? Faster, not better. Better is a bonus.

  2. 2

    Cut

    Eliminate before you automate. If nobody would notice a task disappeared, kill it. Automating waste is waste that runs faster.

  3. 3

    Build

    Context before tools. Small blocks, tested one at a time. Boring wins.

Part 4 · Setting it up

Day 1 is one word.

/onboard

Seven questions. Fifteen minutes. Answer them out loud if that is easier.

At the end it writes your files and your AIOS knows who you are.

What it asks

Who you are and what you sell. How you write. Your next 90 days. Where the money lands. Where you talk to people. Where the files live. What eats your week.

Part 4 · Setting it up

The one rule I will not bend

Paste your writing. Do not type it.

When it asks for two things you have written, open a real email and paste it raw. Typos included.

Anything you compose inside the chat is already shaped by the chat. Do that and you teach it a voice that is not yours, and every draft after that sounds like a stranger wearing your name.
