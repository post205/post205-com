01

The 60-Second Summary

All five lenses reject hiring capacity as the binding constraint, and the reason is the same one that sank the two shapes examined before it: headcount is what an owner counts, not what limits them. The decisive evidence is dispersion — within narrow industries the 90th-percentile firm produces roughly 1.92× the output of the 10th from the same measured inputs, so identical staffing cannot be the cap. Meanwhile a US lawyer bills about 37% of an eight-hour day, which means most service firms are sitting on unsold capacity they already pay for. What binds is sold hours: demand converted into scheduled, paid work. The uncomfortable half of this briefing is what the evidence does not support. The case that management systems relieve the constraint rests on two randomised trials — seventeen textile firms in India and 432 SMEs in one Mexican city — neither in services, neither in Southeast Asia, one with a null first-year employment result. The diagnosis is well founded. The cure is not, and this briefing declines to pretend otherwise.

02

Five Key Findings, Ranked by Reliability

Identical inputs, near-double output — dispersion rules headcount out
1
Reliability: Peer-reviewed survey of a large literature
8/10

Within four-digit US industries, the average gap in logged total factor productivity between the 90th and 10th percentile plant is 0.651 — a ratio of 1.92× output from the same measured inputs. In China and India the average 90-10 ratio exceeds 5:1. If labour were the binding constraint, output conditional on headcount would cluster; it does the opposite. Two verified caveats that must travel with this number: the estimate is defined on manufacturing plants, and service-sector dispersion is measured far less well. The measure is also revenue-based TFP, so it partly captures demand and price rather than pure capability.

2
Reliability: Large proprietary datasets, vendor-published
7/10

Two measures, reconciled rather than averaged. Against a fixed eight-hour day, US lawyers bill 37% — about 2.9 hours, roughly 1,693 billable hours a year — in a dataset skewed to solo and small firms. Against available hours of delivery staff only, mid-to-large professional services firms average 66.4%, the lowest in that benchmark’s history, versus a 75% high-performer level. For a small firm the 37% figure is the honest frame: adding a person buys eight hours of cost and about three hours of revenue.

Hiring follows capability rather than causing growth
3
Reliability: Two RCTs, both small and outside services
6/10

Subsidised consulting to 432 SMEs in Puebla, Mexico produced no first-year employment effect but +57% employees and +72% wage bill over 2010–14. A companion trial raised adoption of 38 management practices from 25.6% to 63.4% and productivity by about 17% in year one. Both point the same way: capability moves first, headcount follows. Corrections applied: the widely-quoted “about 50%” is the abstract’s rounding of +57%/+72%; only 57% of firms matched into administrative records; and the claim that headcount was “unchanged” overstates a null result (point estimate −1.28, standard error 6.19) in a sample of seventeen firms.

Apprenticeship is one instrument pointed either way. Structured training manufactured supply for the large law firms; guild rules withheld it — in seventeenth-century Paris, a city of nearly half a million, the metal-engravers’ guild permitted a maximum of 20 masters and the clockmakers 72. That the constraint is work trapped in one senior person’s head, and that systematising it is the fix

This is the historian’s and practitioner’s reading, and it is the most commercially convenient conclusion available to whoever commissioned this research. It should therefore clear a higher bar, and it does not. The Cravath mechanism turns out to be a later scholarly interpretation rather than a documented rationale. The two intervention trials are outside services, outside Southeast Asia, and small. Nothing located here measures service-sector management consulting in a comparable economy. The claim may well be true; it is currently an argument, not a finding, and the page built from this briefing marks it accordingly.

03

The Hidden Connection

The economist says small service firms are largely price-takers, so raising price is not freely available. The academic says demand accumulation, not capacity, explains why young firms grow slowly. Read separately, one is about pricing power and the other about customer bases.

Together they close the exits. A service firm has three levers: hire more, charge more, or sell more of the hours it already owns. Hiring adds cost immediately and revenue slowly. Price is constrained by competition. That leaves the third, and it is the one nobody measures — because unsold hours generate no invoice, no complaint, and no line in any report.

The constraint is invisible precisely because it produces nothing. An empty hour leaves no trace, so the owner counts the thing that does: people.

The assumption this briefing rests on (and the missing 6th lens)

Every lens assumed a firm that wants to grow. The skeptic surfaced the strongest challenge to that: the typical small business is non-innovative, plans to stay small, and was founded for non-pecuniary reasons. The missing lens is the owner who has already chosen enough — for whom a 37% billable day is not waste but the margin that makes the business livable. For that owner every finding here is technically correct and practically irrelevant, and a workshop that treats their choice as a defect is insulting them.

There is also no Philippine causal evidence in this briefing at all. The dispersion work is US manufacturing, the trials are India and Mexico, the utilisation data is US, and the historical cases are American and European. What is genuinely Philippine here is regulatory and statistical — wage orders, contribution rates, establishment counts. The mechanism is imported; only the cost floor is local.

04

What To Actually Do Differently

For a Philippine owner-operator selling labour who believes they need to hire.

01
Run the free-staff test before you hire anyone.

Ask what would happen if five trained people arrived tomorrow at no cost. If revenue would not rise, the constraint is demand. If revenue rises but profit does not, it is price. If it rises and then decays within six months, it is your own management capacity. Hiring only answers the case where none of those is true.

02
Measure sold hours as a share of paid hours, for one month.

Most firms have never calculated it. If a US lawyer bills 2.9 hours of eight, the number in your business is probably lower than you would guess, and moving it costs no wages at all.

03
Price the hire fully before deciding it is affordable.

Basic pay plus roughly 21.75% statutory loading, against payroll clearing every fifteen days while clients pay far later. Each hire is about two months of wages funded from your own working capital before any of it returns.

04
Treat “we can’t find people” as a claim to be tested.

Firms in the same city drawing on the same applicant pool run very different retention. Before concluding the market is empty, check what the offer looks like from the applicant’s side.

05
Be sceptical of anyone selling the fix, including us.

The evidence that management systems relieve this constraint comes from two trials outside services and outside this region, one with a null first-year result. That is a reason to try things and measure them, not a reason to buy a programme on the strength of the research.

  • Within four-digit US manufacturing industries, the 90th-percentile plant produces about 1.92× the output of the 10th from the same measured inputs.
  • US lawyers bill about 37% of an eight-hour day (~2.9 hours; ~1,693 hours a year).
  • Mid-to-large professional services firms average 66.4% billable utilisation of delivery staff, against a 75% high-performer level.
  • PH statutory loading is about 21.75% on a ₱25,000 salary; SSS employer share 10% of MSC (₱5,000–₱35,000), PhilHealth employer 2.5%, Pag-IBIG 2% capped ₱200.
  • The NCR non-agriculture minimum wage is ₱695 under Wage Order NCR-26.
  • Philippines 2024: 1,241,476 establishments, 1,236,908 (99.63%) MSMEs, 90.66% micro, MSMEs 66.58% of employment.
  • IT-BPM 2025: US$40.3B revenue, 1.89M FTEs; 2028 targets cut to $43.3–50.5B and 1.85–2.14M.
  • Seventeenth-century Paris capped metal-engravers at 20 masters and clockmakers at 72.

⚠ Say with a caveat

  • The 1.92× dispersion figure — manufacturing, revenue-based TFP, and services are measured worse.
  • The consulting trials — name the countries and the sample sizes, or they sound like general evidence.
  • “+57% employees over five years” — single city, 57% administrative match rate, imprecisely estimated.
  • Utilisation figures — always say which denominator, and that neither dataset includes PH firms.
  • Arthur Andersen’s ~85,000 staff — press-sourced; and the conviction was reversed on defective jury instructions, not innocence.

✕ Don’t assert

  • “The minimum wage is ₱755.” NCR-27 is enjoined; ₱695 remains enforceable.
  • “IT-BPM attrition runs 30–40%.” No primary source. Every trace is SEO or vendor content.
  • “McDonald’s cut waits from 30 minutes to 30 seconds.” Marketing, blog citing blog. No primary source gives any service-time figure.
  • “Flexner closed 148 medical schools down to 66.” Untraceable; the paper says roughly 160 to 70.
  • “McKinsey adopted up-or-out in 1951.” It was 1954.
  • “Cravath invented leverage.” A 1991 scholarly label, not his rationale.
  • “Bill 3× your loaded cost.” One consultancy’s rule of thumb with no disclosed sample.
  • “Research shows management systems fix this.” Two trials, neither in services, neither in Southeast Asia.
05

The Frontier Question

In a Philippine service SME, what share of paid hours is actually sold — and would the owner change anything if they knew?

The number does not exist. No Philippine dataset reports billable utilisation for small service firms, and the two international figures available measure different things in different countries. Yet everything here turns on it: if a typical firm sells 40% of what it pays for, the entire hiring conversation is misplaced. The measurement is cheap — one month of honest timekeeping — and almost nobody does it, because unsold hours produce no invoice to notice. Answering it for even fifty Philippine firms would be a genuine contribution, and it would tell us whether this framework describes the local market or merely imports one.
