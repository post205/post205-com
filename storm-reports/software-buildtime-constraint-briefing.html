Is “the time before it works at all” the binding constraint in a software business?

A five-lens synthesis: practitioner, academic, skeptic, economist, and historian. Every claim independently checked against its primary source before publication.

Date  4 September 2026 Method  5 author-built expert lenses + contradiction map Audience  Philippine SME owner building software to sell
Verified 16 citation clusters independently checked against primary sources. Result: 2 false, 7 corrected, 1 untraceable. The decisive test — sales and marketing versus R&D in public filings — was confirmed to the cent across three companies.

How to read this

  • The panel was author-constructed. Where all five lenses agree, treat it as a strong hypothesis, not independent proof.
  • Widely-quoted startup statistics were excluded on purpose. “90% of startups fail” has no methodological source, and the standard software-project failure figures were shown two decades ago not to reflect reality. Neither appears in the findings.
  • This shape may not describe the room. See the missing 6th lens: most Philippine software firms bill for delivery rather than sell a product.
01

The 60-Second Summary

Duration is not a scarce quantity, and we have the natural experiment to prove it: the cost and time of building software collapsed over two decades and failure rates did not fall. When cloud infrastructure cut the cost of a first experiment, investors wrote 15–27% smaller first cheques, took 14% fewer board seats, and the firms they funded became more likely to fail — the signature of an information problem, not a time problem. Meanwhile the money in working software companies goes somewhere the founder’s intuition does not expect: Salesforce spent 34.5% of revenue on sales and marketing against 14.4% on R&D; HubSpot 44.1% against 28.9%; Asana 51.5% against 38.1%, still losing $189M a year on a product that has worked for a decade. Building is a fixed cost paid once. Distribution is a recurring cost paid per customer forever, and it is the larger number. The constraint is the cost of being found, and the runway to pay it. For a Philippine firm with almost no venture capital and MSME lending at 4.52% of bank portfolios, that runway is the whole question.

Five Key Findings, Ranked by Reliability

Working software companies spend more on being found than on building
1
Reliability: Three SEC filings, verified to the cent
9/10

Sales and marketing against research and development, as a share of revenue: Salesforce FY2026 34.5% vs 14.4% ($14.345B against $5.993B on $41.525B). HubSpot FY2025 44.05% vs 28.9%, on an 83.76% gross margin — most of that margin goes to acquisition. Cheaper building produced more failure, not less
2
Reliability: Peer-reviewed, identified natural experiment
9/10

Using the arrival of cloud infrastructure in 2006 as a shock across 8,961 venture-backed firms and 2,815 investors: first-round capital in affected segments fell 15–27% (roughly $670k to $1.3M), funds made about 1.5 more investments every two years, board-seat probability dropped 14% — and affected firms were less likely to receive follow-on funding and more likely to fail ultimately. If build time were binding, relaxing it would have raised survival. It lowered it. What the collapse in build cost bought was more shots and faster abandonment, which is what an information constraint looks like.

Supported byAcademic (Ewens, Nanda & Rhodes-Kropf, Journal of Financial Economics 128(3), 2018)
Challenged byNo one
A finished product with no channel dies anyway
3
Reliability: Court findings and a settlement, both primary
8/10

The United States v. Microsoft findings of fact state it directly: pre-installation by manufacturers and bundling by internet providers “lead most efficiently to browser usage,” and “no other distribution channel for browsing software even approaches the efficiency” of them. BeOS was technically admired and technically finished; Be’s claim was that manufacturers were barred from dual-booting it, and Microsoft paid $23,250,000 net of fees to settle in September 2003. The company did not survive. In the other direction, DOOM shipped its first episode free for anyone to copy in December 1993 and sold the rest by mail order — the distribution mechanism was the business, the engine was the product.

Tooling has not made the build stretch the answer
4
Reliability: One small RCT plus an inconclusive follow-up
6/10

Sixteen experienced open-source developers working 246 real tasks in their own mature repositories were 19% slower with AI assistance (confidence interval +2% to +39%) while estimating afterwards that they had been 20% faster. Corrections: the 24% figure often quoted is the pre-study forecast, not the post-hoc estimate. And the follow-up must not be cited as established speedup — with 57 developers it reported −18% and −4%, but both confidence intervals cross zero, and the authors themselves call the results unreliable and are redesigning the experiment. The authors also do not claim AI fails to help developers generally. The argument does not need it to: an input that is getting cheaper cannot be the thing that binds.

That Philippine SMEs do not buy software

A Philippine government baseline survey put advanced digital tool use among MSMEs at around 6%, with 23% reporting no digitalisation at all and about half using only basic tools. If that holds, a local software firm faces a market that has to be created before it can be sold to. But the figures come from a survey of 400 firms conducted in 2021, and this briefing initially attributed them to an entirely different publication — a multi-country brief which, on extraction, contains neither number. Directionally plausible, weakly sourced, and four years old in a market that moved through a pandemic. Do not build an argument on the precise percentages.

03

The Hidden Connection

The economist says distribution is the recurring cost and building is the fixed one. The historian says the constraint has moved in one direction across every era of the industry — building cheaper, distribution more contested. The academic says cheaper experiments produced more abandonment. Three different methods, one shape.

App stores are the cleanest demonstration. They removed the gatekeeper and collapsed distribution cost to nearly nothing, and demand did not follow: the overwhelming majority of apps became findable only by someone who already knew the name. Shelf space became infinite; attention did not. Every technology that made building or shipping cheaper has increased the number of finished products competing for a quantity of attention that did not grow.

Which means the founder who says “it just needs to be finished” is naming the one input that fifty years of progress has already relieved, and ignoring the one that has been getting harder the whole time.

The assumption this briefing rests on (and the missing 6th lens)

Every lens assumed a firm building a product to sell repeatedly. In the Philippines that is the rare case. IT and software services is roughly a sixth of the IT-BPM sector — billed delivery, not licensed product — and local firms typically fund a product out of services cash rather than instead of it. That removes the shape’s premise: the pre-revenue stretch is subsidised, so it is not unfunded, so it does not bind. What binds instead is the founder’s week, because paid client work always outranks unpaid product work, and the same person does both.

The missing lens is the services firm with a product on the side, which is what most Philippine software SMEs actually are. For them the useful question is not how long the build takes but how many hours a week survive contact with billable work — a Service-shape question wearing a Software label. It is entirely possible that Software is a shape almost nobody in a Philippine SME room genuinely has.

04

What To Actually Do Differently

For a Philippine owner building software who believes the problem is finishing it.

01
Decide whether you are a product business at all.

If services revenue funds the build and the same person sells both, you are running a service business with a product attached. The constraint is your calendar, not your codebase, and the Service page is the one to read.

02
Price the cost of being found before you finish building.

Mature software companies spend a third to a half of revenue on it, permanently. If you have not costed acquisition, you have costed the cheap half of the business.

03
Count runway in customers, not months.

At around 16 months’ median payback on acquisition, the question is not whether you can finish but whether you can fund the gap between spending to win a customer and being repaid by them — with almost no local venture capital and MSME lending at 4.52% of bank portfolios.

04
Sell it before it works.

The evidence says the scarce input is information about who pays, and that it can be bought before the build rather than after. A signed intent from a paying customer is worth more than a finished feature, and it arrives sooner.

05
Distrust the failure statistics, including ours.

“90% of startups fail” has no methodological source. The standard software-project failure figures were shown two decades ago to be built on definitions that do not reflect reality. Product-market fit has no validated instrument at all. Anyone quoting precise numbers at you about why software companies die is repeating folklore.

What’s Safe to Assert

✓ Safe — verified against primary source

  • Salesforce FY2026: sales and marketing 34.5% of revenue, R&D 14.4%.
  • HubSpot FY2025: 44.05% versus 28.9%, on an 83.76% gross margin.
  • Asana FY2026: 51.5% versus 38.1%, net loss $189.024M.
  • Cheaper experimentation cut first-round cheques 15–27% and board seats 14%, and raised ultimate failure.
  • “No other distribution channel for browsing software even approaches the efficiency of OEM pre-installation and IAP bundling” — US v. Microsoft findings of fact.
  • Microsoft paid Be Inc. $23,250,000 net of fees, September 2003.
  • DOOM released 10 December 1993, first episode free and freely redistributable.
  • Philippine banks lent 4.52% (₱488.13B) of a ₱10.8T portfolio to MSMEs as of June 2024.
  • Sixteen experienced developers were 19% slower with AI assistance on 246 real tasks.

⚠ Say with a caveat

  • The 10% MSME lending mandate — it lapsed in 2025; do not describe it as a live obligation.
  • Median CAC payback of 16 months — from an industry benchmark survey, n=342.
  • The METR result — small sample, and the authors do not claim AI fails to help generally.
  • Philippine startup funding — always say which definition; the two published figures count different things.
  • “Zombie” app shares — the metric rises mechanically as the store grows against fixed top-list slots.
  • Product-market fit — say “no standard operationalisation and no validated instrument,” not “no validated construct.”

✕ Don’t assert

  • “IBM unbundled software in 1969.” Operating-system code stayed free with the hardware; only services, education and application programs were unbundled.
  • “Philippine startup funding collapsed from $1.12B to $120M.” A definitional gap, not a trend.
  • “90% of startups fail.” No methodological source exists.
  • “42% fail from no market need.” Self-reported post-mortems from a self-selected sample, not a failure rate.
  • The standard software-project success and failure rates. Shown in 2010, against 5,457 forecasts on 1,211 projects, not to reflect reality.
  • “The 40% rule tells you when you have product-market fit.” Never validated in peer-reviewed research.
  • “Only 6% of Philippine MSMEs use advanced digital tools” as a current fact. A 400-firm survey from 2021, and we mis-attributed it once already.
  • The CAC payback figures by contract size. Sub-$5K is 11 months, not 8; $50–100K is 22, not 24.
05

The Frontier Question

How many Philippine software SMEs are actually product businesses, and how many are services firms with a product on the side?

Nobody has counted. The distinction decides everything: a services firm with a product is constrained by the founder’s week, a product firm by runway against acquisition cost, and the advice diverges completely. National statistics record the sector by revenue and headcount, not by business model, and the firms themselves have an incentive to describe services work as product work when raising money or recruiting. Until someone counts, a workshop teaching seven shapes to a Philippine room is guessing about which one these owners are in — and on the evidence here, the honest answer may be that Software belongs in the deck mainly so the two or three people who genuinely have it can find themselves.
