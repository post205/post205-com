Is utilisation the binding constraint in an asset/rental business?

A five-lens synthesis: practitioner, academic, skeptic, economist, and historian. Every claim independently checked against its primary source before publication.

Date  31 August 2026 Method  5 author-built expert lenses + contradiction map Audience  Philippine SME owner-operator letting units, vehicles or equipment
Verified All 20 citation clusters independently checked against primary sources on 31 Aug 2026. Result: 3 false, 10 corrected, 7 untraceable or unverified. Two historical cases were dropped entirely rather than published unchecked.

How to read this

  • The panel was author-constructed. All five lenses share one framing, so where they agree, treat it as a strong hypothesis, not independent proof.
  • Confidence is scored 1-10 on evidence quality: peer-reviewed causal work > official policy/financial data > single commissioned survey > analogy > vendor content.
  • The lead evidence in the first draft did not survive. A widely repeated “operators target 65–75% utilisation” benchmark turned out to be vendor marketing with no association or filer behind it. It has been replaced with one verified operator figure.
01

The 60-Second Summary

All five lenses reject the proposition, and the academic lens rejects it on a definitional ground rather than an empirical one: a firm that chooses its vacancy rate is, by construction, not constrained by it. Natural vacancy rate theory (Rosen & Smith, AER 1983; Gabriel & Nothaft, JUE 2001, both verified exactly) holds that rents move with deviation from a market-specific equilibrium vacancy, not from zero — so a landlord at full occupancy is under-priced rather than optimised. Hertz, after two decades of fleet optimisation, averaged 81% utilisation in FY2025 by choice. What actually binds is the capital position: the asset you bought, where you put it, the terms you financed it on, and the fact that it decays whether or not anyone is renting it. In Metro Manila this is not abstract — gross secondary yields near 4.8% sit roughly 2.7 points below the 7.544% average bank lending rate, so a levered unit loses money at 100% occupancy. The contested question is whether Philippine buyers are therefore underwriting appreciation rather than yield; the evidence for that is stronger than this briefing first assumed.

Five Key Findings, Ranked by Reliability

Landlords hold vacancy on purpose — full occupancy means under-pricing
1
Reliability: Peer-reviewed, published, verified exactly
9/10

Real rent change is driven by the deviation of observed vacancy from a market-specific natural rate — the vacancy at which real rents hold constant. That equilibrium level differs systematically by metro: duration of vacancy varies with local housing costs and stock heterogeneity, incidence with mobility and population growth. The consequence is that occupancy is an outcome a firm selects along a price/occupancy frontier, not a ceiling it runs into.

That Philippine condo landlords are really underwriting appreciation, with rent as a carry subsidy

The economist lens argues that because yield sits below borrowing cost, anyone buying is implicitly betting on capital gains — making this leveraged land speculation with a tenant attached, constrained by the refinancing window rather than the vacancy rate. This briefing initially treated that as refuted and it is not. The correction: The Rate of Return on Everything does not report a ~1% real housing capital gain; its Table 6 gives nominal housing capital gains of 5.72% against rental income of 5.49%, a roughly even split, with capital gains far more volatile (SD 10.42 vs 2.02). Philippine RPPI data for Q4 2025 shows condominiums up 3.5% year-on-year while houses rose 0.1%. So the appreciation story is live. What is well supported is only that yield is the steadier component — not the larger one.

03

The Hidden Connection

The academic lens says utilisation is a chosen variable and therefore cannot bind. The historian says rental businesses die at full occupancy when their funding fails. These look like different objections — one theoretical, one about catastrophe.

They meet at the balance sheet. Everything an operator can still decide after purchase — price, tenant, turnover, marketing — moves the small residual left after the loan is paid. Everything that actually determines survival — what was bought, where, at what price, on what terms, and how fast it wears out — was fixed on the day of purchase and cannot be revisited. A rental business is a decision made once and then operated inside.

This is why the operator’s daily metric and the business’s fate are almost unrelated. Occupancy is the number you can move; it is not the number that decides.

The assumption this briefing rests on (and the missing 6th lens)

Every lens assumed a financed, formal operator. The NAA cost structure is mortgaged US apartment stock; the Hertz and GPA cases are leveraged corporates; the yield-versus-lending-rate argument only bites if you borrowed. The missing lens is the unlevered Filipino owner — someone who inherited land, built rooms from savings or remittances, and carries no loan at all. For them the 46-cent mortgage line is zero, the refinancing window does not exist, and a 4.8% yield on capital they were never charged for is not a loss. That describes a large share of Philippine boarding-house and apartment operators, and none of the five lenses modelled it.

There is also no Philippine causal research here at all. The natural-vacancy result is US, the cost structure is US, the failures are US, UK and Irish. The local material is market commentary and regulatory text. A Philippine study could shift the conclusion without contradicting a single citation in this briefing.

04

What To Actually Do Differently

For a Philippine owner letting units, vehicles or equipment who believes the problem is empty inventory.

01
Track collection efficiency, not occupancy.

Pesos received over pesos billed, per unit, per month. A fully occupied building of non-payers reads as 100% on the metric you are watching and zero on the one that matters, and Philippine ejectment has no published disposition timetable to rescue you.

02
Put your yield next to your borrowing rate before anything else.

If gross yield is below the loan rate, occupancy is not the problem and no amount of filling fixes it. Metro Manila secondary yields near 4.8% against a 7.544% average lending rate is a structural gap, not an operating one.

03
If your rents are at or below ₱10,000, treat vacancy as your only repricing lever.

Under the current rent-control extension, increases for a sitting tenant are capped at 2.3% for 2025 and 1% for 2026 — but on vacancy you may set the next tenant’s opening rent freely. Above ₱10,000 a month there is no cap at all. Turning over a long-standing below-market tenant can be the most profitable act available, which is the reverse of what maximising occupancy implies.

04
Name who pays when it wears out.

Net returns on long-run property data run near 2.3% real against gross yields near 5% — the difference is the cost of keeping the asset alive. Every lease structure either keeps that obligation with you or moves it to the tenant, and that choice outlives any occupancy decision.

05
Ask whether you are actually betting on the price of the asset.

If the rent does not cover the borrowing, the return has to come from resale. That is a defensible position, but it is a different business from letting property, and it is constrained by refinancing and timing rather than by tenants.

What’s Safe to Assert

✓ Safe — verified against primary source

  • Rents move with deviation from a market-specific natural vacancy rate, not from zero (Rosen & Smith 1983; Gabriel & Nothaft 2001).
  • Hertz averaged 81% utilisation in FY2025 and 78% in Q4 — by choice (SEC 8-K).
  • Hertz filed Chapter 11 on 22 May 2020 with $18.75bn of debt; the trigger was a fleet-financing amortisation event.
  • Metro Manila Q4 2025: Bay Area vacancy 57.3%, Ortigas Center 6.4%.
  • PH rent control: 2.3% cap for 2025, 1% for 2026, sitting tenants only; uncapped on vacancy; no cap at all above ₱10,000/month.
  • NYC rents were frozen on 1 November 1943 at 1 March 1943 levels.
  • PH RPPI Q4 2025: +1.6% year-on-year overall; condominiums +3.5%, houses +0.1%.

⚠ Say with a caveat

  • Metro Manila yields of 4.1%/4.8% — gross, Q4 2025, a named brokerage estimate, not official data.
  • Vacancy of ~25% and ~8 years’ absorption — a brokerage forecast, not a government statistic.
  • The 46-cent mortgage share — US, financed, 5+ unit properties on Freddie Mac loans. Illustration, not a local benchmark.
  • 7.544% lending rate — BSP data reached via CEIC; it is the UKB average lending rate, not a mortgage rate.
  • NYC abandonment of ~38,000 units a year — attributed to RAND but traced only through a secondary history.
  • Philippine ejectment taking months to over a year — practitioner estimate; no official duration data exists.

✕ Don’t assert

  • “Operators target 65–75% utilisation; above 80% means under-fleeted.” Vendor marketing only. No association or public filer publishes such a band.
  • “Car rental runs at 66.2% industry-wide.” Untraceable; appears only in stat aggregators citing each other.
  • “Long-run real housing capital gains are only ~1% a year.” Not in the paper. Nominal capital gains and rental income are roughly even.
  • “Rental yield is the larger component of housing return.” False in nominal terms. It is the steadier component.
  • “Rent control covers ₱10,000 in HUCs and ₱5,000 elsewhere.” That is the superseded statutory text. The operative resolution sets one nationwide ₱10,000 threshold.
  • “200,000+ NYC units abandoned in the 1960s–70s.” Traces only to advocacy commentary.
  • “A published replication comment refutes the collateral channel.” No such published comment exists; the disputes are unpublished working papers and the authors have rebutted.
  • “GE took over GPA’s fleet.” It bought 44 of 464 aircraft and took over management.
05

The Frontier Question

What share of Philippine rental units are operated unlevered — and does the constraint change entirely for those owners?

Every finding here assumes a loan. Take the loan away and the largest cost line vanishes, the yield-versus-lending-rate gap stops mattering, and the refinancing window disappears. A large but unmeasured share of Philippine boarding houses, apartments and tricycles are owned outright, built from savings or remittances on inherited land. For those operators the binding constraint might genuinely revert to occupancy, or move to maintenance, or to the opportunity cost of capital they never priced. No lens addressed it and no dataset answers it, which means the framework currently speaks confidently to the financed minority and guesses at the rest.
